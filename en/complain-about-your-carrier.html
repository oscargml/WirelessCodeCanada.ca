How to Complain About Rogers, Bell or Telus — and Actually Get a Refund

Whether it's a surprise overage, a fee that shouldn't exist, or service that never worked, the process is the same for every Canadian carrier. Done right, it's free and it can put real money back in your pocket.

The CCTS — the independent complaints body — can order your provider to repay proven losses up to $5,000 per complaint. It's free to use.

Step 1 — Complain to your carrier first (and get it in writing)

The CCTS requires you to give your provider a chance to fix it first. Contact them, state the problem in one or two sentences, and say what you want (a refund, a correction, a cancellation without fees).

  • Rogers: Office of the President / escalations via 1-888-ROGERS1 or online chat.
  • Bell: Bell's escalation team / "Office of the President" after front-line support.
  • Telus: Team of Customers First / escalations line after first contact.

Always get a ticket or reference number and note the date, the agent, and what was promised. This is your evidence.

Step 2 — Escalate to the CCTS (free)

If the carrier refuses, stalls, or doesn't fix it within about two weeks, file with the CCTS. The fastest route is their online complaint form. You'll describe the issue, list what you've already tried, and state your desired outcome.

Step 3 — Send a written, cited demand

Carriers respond faster to a clear letter that references the specific rule they broke (the Wireless Code, the Internet Code, or the fee ban). You don't need a lawyer — our free tool builds the letter for you:

What the CCTS can order — and what it can't

It handles contract disputes, billing errors, service-delivery failures, and credit-management issues, and can award compensation up to $5,000. It does not set prices or handle pure "I want a better deal" requests — for that, your leverage is switching (now fee-free).

Common issues worth complaining about

  • Data overage or roaming charges beyond the Wireless Code caps
  • A cancellation or switching fee (now banned)
  • Being told your phone can't be unlocked (it must be, free)
  • A plan or price that changed mid-contract without your agreement
